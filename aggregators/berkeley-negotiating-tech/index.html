Negotiating Tech: An Inventory of U.S. Union Contract Provisions for the Digital Age

Worker powerAI governing laborsearchable inventoryactive
Negotiating Tech: An Inventory of U.S. Union Contract Provisions for the Digital Age: a searchable inventory. Operator: UC Berkeley Labor Center (Center for Labor Research and Education), Technology and Work Program; researched and compiled by Lisa Kresge. Its unit: a technology-related provision of a U.S. collective bargaining agreement (a direct excerpt or a paraphrased summary), linked to the full contract and coded by bargaining strategy, technology type and function, worker and union rights, and employer obligations. Whose labor: the work of union-represented workers in public and private sectors across a range of industries, where employers introduce or use workplace technologies.

Receives the gains, bears the costsnot recorded as outcomes; the provisions record negotiated guarantees around job security, pay, retraining and working conditions during technological change

What it records

IntersectionsWorker power: bargaining, organizing, consent, refusal, contestation and enforceable protections; AI governing labor: hiring, surveillance, scheduling, quotas, evaluation and discipline
Kind of evidencenegotiated provision
Unit of recorda technology-related provision of a U.S. collective bargaining agreement (a direct excerpt or a paraphrased summary), linked to the full contract and coded by bargaining strategy, technology type and function, worker and union rights, and employer obligations op
Whose laborthe work of union-represented workers in public and private sectors across a range of industries, where employers introduce or use workplace technologies
Methodcontract text: over 500 agreements gathered from the OLMS and OPM databases, union and employer websites and union contributions; keyword search followed by manual review; each provision coded in a structured framework op
Coveragegeography: United States; period: contracts negotiated across several decades; last updated 2 July 2025
What it findsmany provisions refer broadly to technology or technological change, which lets one clause cover diverse technologies; a few recent contracts explicitly reference artificial intelligence or generative AI; most do not name AI; specific technology language is more common in clauses governing how particular technologies are used, often with participatory oversight or joint decision-making; long-standing provisions remain relevant to the digital workplace
Its stated limitsan informational resource; it offers no model language and no best practices; not exhaustive of all sectors; many agreements do not name specific technologies, so keyword searches by technology miss them; references to specific employers or unions are generally omitted from the provision text

What this evidence can settle

Can answer

  • what U.S. unions have negotiated on technology: notice, information, participation, job security, pay, retraining, monitoring, data rights, telework
  • how contract language names or leaves unnamed specific technologies, AI included

Cannot answer

  • whether the provisions are enforced or what they change at work
  • how common any provision is across all U.S. contracts
  • bargaining outside the United States

In the worked questions

  • Where are actual negotiated protections against automated monitoring and discipline?

Where it meets Labor Vector

rd The inventory records negotiated limits on how employers direct work through technology; it meets the directionality stack at the contract layer, where workers hold rights to notice, negotiation and joint oversight. It holds no measure of task fidelity or user labor drag.
