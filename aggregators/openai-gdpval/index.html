GDPval: a benchmark (an evaluation dataset with an experimental automated grader). Operator: OpenAI: the paper's 19 authors (Tejal Patwardhan first; nine marked equal contribution) give OpenAI as their affiliation, with correspondence to an openai.com address; OpenAI's own post at this address answered 403 and is unread. Its unit: a task: a request (often with reference files) and a deliverable, constructed from actual work product of an expert professional; graded by blinded pairwise expert comparison of model and human deliverables (win rate). Whose labor: the work product of recruited industry professionals in 44 predominantly digital occupations across the 9 sectors contributing over 5% of U.S. GDP, recast as tasks for models; the same professionals' labor as task writers, reviewers and graders.

We introduce GDPval, a benchmark evaluating AI model capabilities on real-world economically valuable tasks.

In its own words: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04374, read 2026-10-29

Who supplies, who directs, who gains

Supplies the laborrecruited industry professionals (minimum 4 years' experience, average 14) wrote the tasks and their deliverables, reviewed them (an average of five reviews per task) and graded model deliverables against human ones; the paper states they were 'well compensated' and gives no rate; it also thanks 'vendor partners' without naming them
Directs itOpenAI's research team: occupations chosen by GDP share and total wages; researchers reviewed all tasks, promoted experts to reviewers and ran quality-control checks; OpenAI models screened submissions, with experts retaining final responsibility for task accuracy
Receives the gains, bears the costsnot recorded as a distribution; the paper models time and cost savings for an expert who uses a model and fixes or redoes unsatisfactory output, valued at BLS median wages, and states those wages likely underestimate the experts' market cost

Each as the resource itself records it. Where it records nothing, the census says so.

IntersectionsLabor performed with AI: assistance, delegation, task fidelity, correction burdens and user labor drag; Labor producing AI: annotation, moderation, evaluation, maintenance, and creative or scholarly work entering training and retrieval

What it findsperformance of OpenAI frontier models increased roughly linearly over time on the gold subset; models are beginning to approach parity with industry experts: 47.6% of Claude Opus 4.1 deliverables were graded better than or as good as the human deliverable; the text also says model deliverables outperformed or matched experts' in just over half the tasks; Claude Opus 4.1 was best overall, strongest on aesthetics; GPT-5 strongest on accuracy; experts most often preferred the human deliverable because models failed to fully follow instructions; with expert review and redo counted, the payoff shrinks: GPT-5 at 39.0% win rate gives 90x naive speed but 1.12x (try once) and 1.39x (try n times); of GPT-5's losses, roughly 29% were rated bad or catastrophic (roughly 3% catastrophic); win rates decline as task completion time increases; reasoning effort, prompting and scaffolding raise performance; OpenAI's official GDPval leaderboard, as Inspect Evals (third party) reports it: 31.9% (wins only) and 34.6% (wins and ties), lower than the 47.3% Inspect Evals obtained for GPT-5 with thinking set to low through OpenAI's automated grader
Its stated limitsdataset size: a limited, initial cut of knowledge work tasks; self-contained computer-based knowledge work only: manual labor, physical tasks, tacit knowledge, proprietary tools and communication between individuals are out of scope; tasks are precisely specified, one-shot and non-interactive; the automated grader has limitations, and agrees less with experts on capable OpenAI models' outputs; cost: industry expert grading is expensive; the speed and cost analysis omits review of human deliverables and the cost of catastrophic mistakes; completion times are experts' self-reports; grading could not be fully blind

What this evidence can settle

Can answer

  • how frontier models' deliverables on expert-written, precisely specified, one-shot occupational tasks compare with experts' deliverables, as judged by blinded expert graders
  • how that comparison varies by model, sector, occupation, file type and task length
  • under its stated scenarios, how much expert time and cost a model could save once review and redo are counted

Cannot answer

  • how work is performed in actual workplaces, where context must be found and tasks are interactive
  • employment or wage outcomes
  • who receives the time and cost savings it models
  • physical, interpersonal or tacit-knowledge work
  • the terms and rate at which the professionals were paid

In the worked questions

  • How do people use AI systems in their own work, and how is that use recorded?

Where it meets Labor Vector

rd The archive's reading: GDPval grades a model's deliverable against an expert's deliverable on a task an expert wrote, a fixed commission, which meets DSL at the question of whether the system's labor fulfils the commissioned task, measured in its own terms of win rate. Its 'try n times, then fix it yourself' accounting counts the expert's review and redo time against the model, which is where it meets user labor drag; task-origin is the experts' own work product, as the paper states it.
